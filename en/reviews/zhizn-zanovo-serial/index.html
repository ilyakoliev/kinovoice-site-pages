18 June 2026

Living Anew (Zhizn zanovo), which was among the things I was looking forward to with interest, has fully lived up to expectations.

The time-travel comedy looks like a very gripping, solid piece of work. Alexander Yatsenko, in the role of an ambulance doctor tired of life and of his own inadequacy, is very convincing, as Arrhythmia showed, and this series uses that advantage 100%. The humour here is charming and unhackneyed, so watching is a great pleasure.

As a result of an MRI machine breaking down, the hero from our time is transported to 2002, and the panorama of St Petersburg in the 2000s, shown to the music of Glukoza (the Russian pop singer), simply takes your breath away. The cinema is showing Progulka (“The Stroll”), and every student has a pager on their belt. And the hero himself finds himself in that time with all the knowledge of his 40 years, which he puts to use in his own way.

A super project, which I will await impatiently and will definitely watch to the end.

4 November 2026

The series Living Anew came out on the streaming platforms suspiciously quietly, and I, as I had planned since Pilot (the Russian series festival), am continuing to watch it.

Everything is done attractively, rhythmically (perfect for watching many episodes in a row!) and without the dreary clichés inherent in the time-travel genre. Not without some confusion in its moral compass, but Dima the doctor (Alexander Yatsenko/Anton Shavrin) in 2002 tirelessly extricates himself from one mishap after another, trying meanwhile not to incur the notorious butterfly effect, and he does it rather wittily. A compliment to the script and the costume designers: the wardrobe of Lenya, the protagonist’s best friend, is so stylish that you want to steal the whole lot for personal use in 2026.

The main advantage, which reveals itself after a few episodes, is the perfect casting of the characters in their youth and in adulthood. Anton Shavrin (the young version of Yatsenko) wonderfully conveys the state of a forty-year-old doctor locked in the body of an 18-year-old student, a real find of the series. Robert Zakharyan, who plays Artyom Tkachenko’s character from 2002, is altogether a most successful comic type; I hope he will reveal himself further in that capacity. And I very much like the heroines in their youth: Alisa and Kristina are played by Elizaveta Ishchenko and Margarita Dyachenkova (whom I have been following for a long time, because we have known each other since school), and each has an elusive intonation carried over from her adult version (Karina Razumovskaya and Elena Podkaminskaya respectively).

I have a few final episodes left for dessert, and I am stretching them out as best I can.
