Moscow Does Not Believe in Tears: An Atmospheric Look at 2003 from 2025

In short

4 September 2025

What strikes you first about episode one of the new Moscow Does Not Believe in Tears (the series sequel to the Oscar-winning 1980 film) is that this is not an exact reconstruction of 2003, the year the three heroines first meet, but rather an atmospheric look at it from our own 2025.

Perhaps that comes with the musical genre, which is full of conventions even without leaps in time, and all the more so with them. Hence the anachronistic details: for example, in 2003 Tina Stojiljković’s and Ivan Yankovsky’s characters dance in the “dry fountain” in Muzeon park, which in fact only opened in 2013.

The main anchors of the Soviet original are still there, in the heroines’ characters and their firm friendship, but otherwise this is a new story. Zhora Kryzhovnikov’s signature style brings the long-awaited jump cuts, the jitteriness and the heroines’ cheerful anger. On first viewing the series feels like something between The Boy’s Word and Kombinatsiya (“Combination”, the series about the 1980s girl band), the two previous high-profile projects from Wink (the Russian streaming service): in the first, the musical inserts worked at 200%, while in the second they tended to replace dialogue in places (and dialogue is sometimes the more interesting thing to listen to, after all).

Since the heroines’ fates are only sketched in over the course of the episode, what touched me most were the signs of the times: a recognisable pre-Sobyanin marshrutka (a minibus taxi from before Moscow’s current mayor), bus stops plastered with ads, loud eclecticism in the clothes. And the pop-culture artefacts and the prices deserve a special mention:

  • Terminator 3 and Lara Croft are showing at the cinema, the Tolkienist movement is in its prime, and the girls dash off to a casting for Fabrika zvyozd (“Star Factory”, the Russian pop talent show). It brings a tear to the eye.
  • A hamburger at McDonald’s costs 17 roubles. An order of two cheeseburgers, fries and ice creams comes to 160 roubles. To make such prices hurt a little less, you can pretend they are simply in today’s Belarusian roubles.

The one thing that is not yet clear is whether the project will stay on the side of fairy-tale sweetness or cross over to realism (especially in the part with the heroines 20 years on); I’ll keep watching to find out.

11 September 2025

I liked the second episode of Moscow Does Not Believe in Tears so much that I am ready to forget all the doubts I had after the first.

The sparing use of musical numbers really suits the series. The second episode has just one such scene, but it is brilliant (literally and figuratively): a properly cool music video inside the show. In this number Anastasia Talyzina and Andrey Burkovsky combine comedy, sincerity and a showy display of how they feel about each other, which matters for an inserted number that takes place, as it were, in the heroine’s head. The key difference this time is that the musical scene does not retell part of the plot (as it did with Ksyusha’s miserable job) but adds to the heroine’s emotional backdrop without affecting the sequence of events in the world of the series! Cool, vivid, clear, unobtrusive.

And the definitive acting triumph belongs to Mash Milash: her heroine is, it seems to me, the most distinctive and tangible, and following her is a great pleasure. The second episode is mostly hers in terms of screen time, so the creators are presumably betting heavily on this character. She truly sets the mood, whether she is spinning a yarn for the American or taking the sweetest historical re-enactor, played by Andrey Maksimov, on an emotional rollercoaster: cruel in both cases, but funny. I want to believe that things will work out with the Tolkienist after all, and that Masha, true to the classics, will ask the American where strength lies (the famous line from Brother 2).

19 September 2025

I keep being reassured that the new Moscow Does Not Believe in Tears gets better with every episode.

The third episode again has just one full-blown musical number, unexpectedly not involving a heroine, plus one half-musical scene with the central characters. Modest and tasteful, as they say, and above all meaningful, with a dramatic foundation under the musical wrapping. I hope things stay sorted in this department right through to the last episode.

As a whole, the events of the episode are in what you might call a state of in progress ❌ The girls have already understood something about their relationships but have not yet had time to come to terms with it, and they keep rolling along the rails laid down in advance. Ksyusha might have got away with the same status too, were it not for her husband, who turned up and brought with him the chilling atmosphere of the phrase “the groom has arrived”. Perhaps the final scene on the balcony rhymes with the moment in the original film when Alentova’s heroine cried herself to sleep in the worst of all possible situations, and after a cut we saw her completely different life years later.

27 September 2025

Having watched episodes 4 and 5 of the new Moscow Does Not Believe in Tears back to back, I understood what the 90 million viewers felt who gave the Soviet film its phenomenal box office. This is how the sources describe the frenzy:

“After the premiere of Moscow Does Not Believe in Tears, huge queues formed outside cinemas, with people waiting for the box offices to open. Screenings began early in the morning and ran late into the night, and people came back to see the film several times”

I, too, wanted to rewatch these two episodes the moment they ended, though I have never felt that urge with the Soviet two-part film. The sense of belonging to a time in which the heroines’ lives are transformed like this strikes a very strong chord, of course. You begin to understand Soviet cinemagoers — especially the women — 100%, and to believe in a miracle within your own era.

The musical numbers in the new episodes are better than ever. I personally find Ksyusha’s transformation into a businesswoman easy to believe, like any cinematic convention that still has a right to exist. Even Katerina’s rise to factory director, incidentally, was met with scepticism in its day: she could not have risen like that without any outside help, people said. Again, I quote a source:

“Moscow could easily have been pulled from distribution and shelved. The film was attacked by critics and the authorities alike, and ‘vulgar melodrama’ was among the mildest charges. There were objections both to the ‘explicit scenes’ and to the very story of a woman’s success. A poor girl like Katya from the provinces could never have had such a career in the USSR — that was the general verdict”

It is especially pleasing that there is no gulf in quality between the two halves of the series; the only thing I will miss is the young versions of the heroines. But the Tolkienist lawyer played by Andrey Maksimov is still here, and his storyline with Masha will definitely remain the most interesting one for me.

3 October 2025

In the second half of the new Moscow Does Not Believe in Tears, I am particularly pleased that my favourite male character has aged the most handsomely.

Andrey Maksimov was treated gently, and 20 years have done his character’s looks nothing but good — well, they certainly haven’t made them worse. The same cannot be said of Ivan Yankovsky’s character, who seemed to be about the same age as the Tolkienist student, so this paradox of ageing is beyond me. And, of course, of Andrey Burkovsky’s, who may have been the oldest of them all but has been aged far too harshly (apparently to make it easier for Olya to leave him for Ruzil Minekaev).

The logic of the ageing (for which, on the whole, great respect to the make-up department) remains a mystery, but I am glad my taste did not let me down.

17 October 2025

In the finale of Moscow Does Not Believe in Tears I can understand and accept everything except the way things end for Masha and Artyom (who seem to have beaten their alcoholism).

As a fan, it hurt (they are my favourite characters). The reason for the break-up is, to my taste, completely unconvincing, and I simply cannot follow Masha’s logic. To me, theirs is the one situation (out of all the couples!) that looks like “just live and be happy”. With both of them working on themselves, of course, but basically problem-free!

The other heroines’ storylines seem more logical to me: they could have been handled differently, or more interestingly, of course, but the outcome we got raises no questions either. On the whole, the finale clearly echoes the start of the series: the friends, as in the good old days, are each other’s only hope, with the whole world against them. Some things, as they say, never change.
