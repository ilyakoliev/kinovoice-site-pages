The Irony of Fate: Myagkov’s Character Now Has an Excuse

In short

“The Irony of Fate, or Enjoy Your Bath!” is a 1976 film directed by Eldar Ryazanov. so there: in my eyes Myagkov’s character from The Irony of Fate now has an excuse, and, while we’re at it, an interesting alternative version in an office setting.

Betsy Isakova·10 January 2024

About the film

Title
The Irony of Fate, or Enjoy Your Bath! · Ирония судьбы, или С лёгким паром!
Director
Eldar Ryazanov
Year
1976

IMDb ↗ · Kinopoisk ↗

since there’s no getting through the New Year holidays without The Irony of Fate (Ironiya sudby; sooner or later it pops up somewhere, even if it wasn’t in your plans), you might as well do some research on the film!

that’s how I ended up at Lev Karakhan’s 1978 article in Iskusstvo kino (“The Art of Cinema”, the Russian film journal), and found something interesting:

We meet Zhenya Lukashin on the day before New Year. He is one of the inhabitants of a modern city of many millions, a panorama of which passes before us at the start of the film. He is “one of” for another reason too: in his standard-issue flat, in his New Year tree set up in the corner of the room, and indeed in his fiancée, we readily recognise the likeness of dozens, hundreds, thousands of identical flats, trees and fiancées. As soon becomes clear, however, “one of” is exactly what Lukashin has not yet become. He is only striving to be, trying to fit into the general order of city life. But he cannot. Something inside Lukashin resists his own aspirations and will not let him merge with that rigidly organised stream of people, divided into neat rivulets, which the filmmakers show on screen and in which everyone hurtles along what seems to be their own route but is in essence a standard, well-worn one.

<…>

Myagkov’s Lukashin is incapable of adapting to the settled standards of modern urban life, yet he sees no other way out for himself either, nor does he look for one. And so he marks time, shifting from foot to foot in indecision, stuck in a position that is strange for this neatly ruled, “standard-issue” world: that of a man who is neither one thing nor the other. It is this very strangeness that gives Myagkov’s performance, and the film as a whole, its distinctive genre character, making the story of the hapless Lukashin now naively touching, now charmingly funny.

<…>

Coming round after his accidental flight, no longer in Moscow but in Leningrad, in Nadya’s flat, Zhenya suddenly discovers that he can express himself openly, with nothing to constrain him any more. Although everything here would seem to be as before (the same standard-issue flat, the same tree, even the fiancée is in a sense the same: Nadya and her intended, Ippolit, are marrying less for love than out of necessity, because the time has come), there is also something in this situation that is, for Lukashin, fundamentally new. The situation is non-standard, and the relationship between Nadya and Zhenya, unexpectedly left alone with each other, takes shape outside anything prescribed or predetermined that weighs on a person, purely according to unwritten rules and laws that no one invented: as their hearts dictate. And this is where the barriers dividing two modern city dwellers come down, and come down literally overnight! Real love comes to them.

I left out of the quotation the points where I don’t quite agree with the author, but what he says about the distinctive kind of hero Myagkov plays is, I think, spot on: a year after The Irony of Fate, in Office Romance (Sluzhebny roman), Myagkov plays Novoseltsev, who is at first so afraid of his boss that he stammers and later falls headlong in love with that same boss, the only difference being that he didn’t have to end up in another city to find love in those very same atypical circumstances, outside the routine template. a curious type of hero it turned out to be!

so there: in my eyes Myagkov’s character from The Irony of Fate now has an excuse, and, while we’re at it, an interesting alternative version in an office setting.
