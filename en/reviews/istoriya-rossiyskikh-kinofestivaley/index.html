A History of Russian Film Festivals: From the 1935 Soviet Film Festival to Kinotavr

In short

And this is where we get to the second-oldest film festival — the Russian one! Or, to be precise, the Soviet Film Festival in Moscow.

Betsy Isakova·15 October 2024·updated 30 October 2024

15 October 2024

All last week I was following Mayak, the Russian film festival in Gelendzhik, and along the way I got curious about the history of the festival movement in Russia. So please welcome a small (but the first on this channel) special project about Russian film festivals! As it happens, I have already written about the cinema where it all began :)

Over the next two weeks, in a series of five posts, I will tell you about the life and death of Russian film festivals from the early 20th century to the present day, lay them out across the calendar year and even try to match them with Western counterparts by the profile of their programmes.

The first part is coming today!

15 October 2024

1️⃣ Part ⅕. The second-oldest

Where did film festivals come from in the first place? It all began with European film clubs in the 1920s; then came curated screenings with programmes that ran for several days; and then, with the arrival of sound in 1927, things sped up many times over. So in 1932 the world’s first film festival took place: Venice. It emerged as part of the Venice Biennale, an international art exhibition held since 1893 (as old as cinema itself!). There was no competition yet; that arrived with the second festival in 1934, when the top prize was not yet the Golden Lion but the Mussolini Cup (yes, cinema was the most important of the arts for all kinds of regimes).

And this is where we get to the second-oldest film festival — the Russian one! Or, to be precise, the Soviet Film Festival in Moscow. 1935, the Udarnik cinema, an international competition, Chapaev as the opening film. On the jury: Sergei Eisenstein, Vsevolod Pudovkin, Alexander Dovzhenko. What made it happen: political hostility to the regime then ruling Italy, and the 15th anniversary of the Soviet film industry, celebrated everywhere in January of that year.

Sadly, neither of the first two festivals flourished for long. Venice went underground for the whole of the Second World War and regained its flagship status only in 1946. And the Soviet Film Festival in Moscow was never held in its original form again. Its successor was, and still is, the Moscow International Film Festival (MIFF). From 1959 it was held every two years, and in 1999 it became annual (who was behind that, by the way, I will explain in one of the next posts). Until 2022 it was Russia’s only A-category film festival (that is, accredited by the International Federation of Film Producers Associations alongside Cannes, Venice, Berlin and a dozen or so other major festivals around the world).

In short, with film festivals everything seems to happen not thanks to circumstances but in spite of them. But when has that ever bothered us? :)

In the next part: who made MIFF annual, what is odd about its programme, which festival split off from the Moscow International, and much more.

2️⃣ A History of Russian Film Festivals. Part ⅖. Changes

So, in the previous part we reached the moment when MIFF became a regular Soviet film festival held every two years. It was an astonishing time: Federico Fellini, Sophia Loren and Francis Ford Coppola came to the USSR just as they did to Venice and Cannes, and in 1987 the jury president was Robert De Niro. In short, MIFF grew by every measure to truly Hollywood proportions.

And by 1988 MIFF had grown so much that its documentary programme split off, taking shape as a separate (and also international!) festival, at that point purely for documentaries: Poslanie k cheloveku (“Message to Man”). At the suggestion of the documentary filmmaker Mikhail Litvyakov, the event moved to Leningrad and was first held there in 1989, and its symbol (in the first photo) was named the “Kentavryonok”, the little centaur (yes, this image hovered over the Russian festival movement even before Kinotavr appeared!). Message to Man is still going strong, and now takes in experimental film, fiction and animation as well. Kinopoisk has a wonderful article with a more detailed history of the festival and unique photographs (I was especially struck by the shot of Leni Riefenstahl receiving an award in 2001)

Meanwhile MIFF began to falter: the collapse of a country, crisis and social upheaval do nobody any good. In 1998 it could not be held at all because of financial problems. In 1999 Nikita Mikhalkov took up the post of MIFF president (something tells me no such post existed before), and the festival has been held annually ever since. A reminder of the context: in 1999 Mikhalkov is a man in triumph, with Burnt by the Sun (the Oscar for Best Foreign Language Film and the Grand Prix of the 47th Cannes Film Festival) and the internationally shot The Barber of Siberia already behind him. So the magical disappearance of the financial problems should come as no surprise, all the more so since various researchers describe the Mikhalkov of that period as a rather effective manager (I relied, in particular, on the book Istoriya rossiyskogo blokbastera, “A History of the Russian Blockbuster”).

In short, MIFF survived hard times for the second time in its history, but other major Russian festivals had already appeared on the horizon: Kinotavr and Okno v Evropu (“Window to Europe”). Where they came from and how they differed — in the next part!

30 October 2024

3️⃣ A History of Russian Film Festivals. Part ⅗. How Kinotavr Got Its Name

So, MIFF survived, and Kinotavr and Window to Europe appeared. Let us take a closer look at them.

Kinotavr did not appear overnight. Its prototype, the Festival of Unsold Films, took place in Podolsk in 1989 on the initiative of Mark Rudinstein, a businessman (if you can call him that; the man has an interesting biography). The thing is, at the height of perestroika the volume of auteur cinema shot up, but cinemas would not buy it, because the new market economy wanted crowd-pleasers, not art films. The result was a mass of films with no chance of reaching cinema screens and audiences. That is where Rudinstein stepped in, giving these films and their makers a chance. The filmmakers’ delight inspired him so much that he decided to stage a bigger festival by the sea — in Sochi, naturally. But unwanted auteur films alone would not do for an event on that scale.

The festival held by the sea in 1991 ended up with a programme in two parts: one for audiences (“Cinema for Everyone”) and one for critics (“Cinema for the Chosen”). They say that this led someone to joke that it was turning into “some kind of centaur” (kentavr). And the film critic Alyona Bokshitskaya corrected them: “Kinotavr!” — giving the festival its name. That is how it would exist for the next 30 years, becoming the symbol of an entire era in film. Incidentally, the post of “festival president” (and we remember who holds it at MIFF) first appeared at Kinotavr, where it was held by Oleg Ivanovich Yankovsky. It sounds honorary, but it served a thoroughly practical purpose: for state sponsorship you needed someone whom officials would be glad to receive in their offices and would not send away empty-handed. For Kinotavr’s first ten years the arrangement with Yankovsky worked, but then something stalled, and the question of selling the festival arose.

In its early days Kinotavr had an international programme (as, in fact, did all the festivals mentioned above). And in 1993 Window to Europe was launched in Vyborg: the oldest national film festival in Russia focused specifically on Russian cinema. A festival with a surprisingly calm history, it outlived Kinotavr and is still running today.
