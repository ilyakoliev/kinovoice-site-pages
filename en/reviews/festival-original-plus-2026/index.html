A Tour of the Gorky Film Studio at Original+: Facts from Film History

In short

Bright and early on the first day of the festival I raced over for a tour of the Gorky Film Studio, where the showcase is being held this year.

Betsy Isakova·18 March 2026·updated 21 March 2026

18 March 2026

Bright and early on the first day of the festival I raced over for a tour of the Gorky Film Studio (a historic Moscow film studio), where the showcase is being held this year. I’m not joking: I wanted to get in so badly that I woke up at 5 a.m. without an alarm. And I really did add a few cool facts from film history to my store of knowledge. I’ll go by the numbers of the photos in the set, because almost every fact has an illustration:

  • Photo 3: this reel holds just 10 minutes of a film. These days, of course, that’s mostly no longer how it works, but every time the sight is a great reminder that cinema is a physical thing. To screen a film like this in a cinema, more than 10 cans of film had to pass through the projectionist’s hands.

  • Photo 4: and this is an editing table. Here they looked at every tiny frame of the film against a backlight, cut it (physically), edited it (that is, literally glued the right stretches of film together) and made all sorts of changes to the film-to-be.

  • Photos 5 and 6: ingenuity truly worthy of a museum. The round bronze pieces of chain mail are nothing other than bits of tin cans (sprats, saury and similar goodies), bent into the right shape and turned label-side in. With no other fish in the sea as far as materials went (forgive the pun), the costume workshop resorted even to tricks like these; to make the point, one of the pieces has been turned to show the very side with the name of the tinned fish.

  • Video and photo 7: a real piece of history, the camera on which Tatyana Lioznova shot Seventeen Moments of Spring (the cult 1973 Soviet spy series). The tour gave me a rounded picture of what everyday work with the camera looked like.

  • Photo 8: to the right of the guide is a hand-held camera (just 5 kg, light as a feather by the standards of the equipment of the day) with the intriguing name Konvas. It was used to shoot the first footage of Earth from space, the 1980 Olympics and many other historic moments. And it was named after its creator, Konstantin Vasilyev, by joining the first syllables of his first name and surname.

  • The most touching fact, though it comes without an illustration. The film The Dawns Here Are Quiet is dedicated to the field nurse Anna Chugunova, who rescued the director Stanislav Rostotsky from the battlefield. At the film’s premiere in 1972, Rostotsky personally talked Anna through the film, since by then, because of her age, she could no longer see well.

21 March 2026

Olya and I have written about the best of the Original+ festival (the Russian series showcase) in a piece for Kinopoisk (Russia’s largest film database, media and streaming service).

I’ve already told you about the charming Pride («Гордость»), which was the one that won; we’ll be watching that to the end. In the piece, be sure to read about The Black Countess (Chyornaya grafinya), coming up from Okko (the Russian streaming service), Call Vitya! («Зовите Витю!») and Pochti nastoyashchy detektiv («Почти настоящий детектив», “An Almost Real Detective”), which are worth the wait.
