Outsource: I Haven’t Enjoyed a Series This Much in a Long Time

In short

“Outsource” is a 2025 series directed by Dushan Gligorov. It’s good that the story has partly come full circle — now Kostya Volkov will head off to a new job at another penal colony and stir things up there.

Betsy Isakova·13 February 2025·updated 1 April 2025

13 February 2025

It’s a pleasure to see a story as cool as Outsource (Autsors) arrive practically at the very start of the TV year: a series with Ivan Yankovsky as Kostya, an enterprising prison warder who, in the mid-1990s, decides to sell victims’ relatives the chance to execute the killer with their own hands. This narrative core is surrounded by the hair-raising stories of the hero’s colleagues and other townspeople, which gives the series a densely packed structure! On that note, I like this alternative poster for the series: Kostya is clearly about to stir up some volcano of consequences.

Judging by the first episodes, Yankovsky’s character works as an infernal, otherworldly force that little by little brings out in the locals hidden traits they never even suspected they had — by asking awkward but very simple questions and offering solutions that are not the most moral, but direct. You can enjoy the character largely as an extension and continuation of Yankovsky’s work as Raskolnikov — only a Raskolnikov who feels he has the right and gives no thought to repentance.

I hope Outsource will set the standard for this year’s other shows too, because so far it’s the strongest thing of 2025.

10 March 2025

How brilliantly the details work in Outsource!

Colleagues singled out the prosecutor’s housewarming scene, but what struck me was the transformation of Natasha’s mother, played by Karina Razumovskaya. The whole time she was handing her daughter destructive advice along the lines of “the main thing is to hang on to a good man”, and behaving not like a mother but like some auntie who is public opinion personified, she never once took off her hat — a symbol of being closed off, insincere, removed from her daughter’s feelings.

But when the irreparable happened in episode 5 (after an abortion she had on her mother’s advice, Natasha ended up in intensive care and will never be able to have children), Natasha’s mother finally took off her protective armour of coldness and constant demands on her daughter. For the first time in the series (it didn’t happen even in the wedding scene!) she appeared before her daughter without the hat and without the airs, even if it came unforgivably late. A small thing, but its effect on the viewer is colossal.

13 March 2025

At some point Outsource started turning into House of the Dragon in its sheer number of intrigues — my soul demanded an episode-by-episode breakdown.

1️⃣ I still have my doubts: does Ivan Yankovsky’s character exist at all? He is some kind of disembodied shadow who manages to be here, there and everywhere, persuading anyone he meets with a hypnotist’s efficiency. I wouldn’t be surprised if in the end he vanished, à la Tyler Durden, leaving the jailers with a heap of unsolvable problems. Especially after the line “Who could possibly do anything to me?”…

2️⃣ The poster with the women turned out to be spot on — judging by episode 6, the ball is passing into their court, and it’s the female half of the characters we should now expect the interesting twists from. Natasha’s mother has put her hat back on, though the only one who has gained in authority is, on the contrary, Natasha herself — which makes her all the more interesting to watch. In fact, all the female characters here are so good that, despite their minimal screen time, I’m ready to praise them separately, over and above my general admiration for the cast.

3️⃣ With each week the episodes fly by faster and faster — given how many plot threads are still tangled, the remaining two episodes have the ultimate resolution in store for us, with answers to every question at once (and surprisingly simple answers at that — as always with Yankovsky’s character). Literally a case of “seven troubles, one answer”, as the Russian saying goes.

15 March 2025

Outsource showrunner Anna Kozlova has given a long interview to Vedomosti (a Russian business daily) — a terribly interesting one, so I’m writing down the main points:

  • We viewers have no idea what Kostya (Ivan Yankovsky) was doing before episode one begins. The show’s makers, though, decided that “Kostya was in Chechnya, so he knows how to kill. And he obviously has PTSD in some form”. That explains a lot, including the elusive resemblance to Danila Bagrov (the hero of Brother, 1997)

  • Kostya has a remarkable character, one that at a different moment in history could have played out quite differently. Anna says outright that “Kostya is ready for anything at all. Had he been born in another time or another country, he could have done something very good for people. But Kostya ended up in the situation he ended up in, and he does what he does”

  • In preparing the series, they studied what the 1990s press wrote about the death penalty as a phenomenon (which, of course, traumatised those who carried it out, too). So the show’s black humour didn’t grow out of nothing but straight out of life: “In one report a journalist asked an executioner: ‘Tell me, please, what are the most interesting things that have happened to you at work?’ And he calmly told it like a funny story: ‘You know, I swear to you, I had this one guy who dropped dead while we were walking him there’”.

  • Natasha (Mila Ershova) may not be such a victim after all: “the heroine herself did everything she could to make what happened to her happen. She flirts with an obviously dangerous man, knowing full well who she is dealing with. She is deliberately playing with fire. And when you play with fire, you have no guarantees. You might get lucky and get away, or you might not.”

  • Anna found a great way to sum up her attitude to the eternal excuse for villainy, “for the sake of the family”, which we come across not only in Outsource but in culture as a whole (think, for example, of how Walter White in Breaking Bad kept harping on that same “for the family” in his day): “Ask anyone ‘why did you do this and that’, and they will say ‘well, I’ve got a mother, I’ve got children, I’ve got a wife — I’m doing it for them’. In other words, you can commit absolute evil while supposedly having nothing to do with any of it yourself; you are always the victim. Naturally, each of the characters wants power and money but isn’t prepared to sacrifice anything. And here is the result”.

21 March 2025

Episode seven of Outsource seems to have shrunk too much (in both running time and incident), and on the whole it looks like a quiet pause in which both sides of the conflict prepare their strategy. Perhaps this haste is needed to bring us inexorably closer to the horrifying denouement (there’s no doubt that is exactly what it will be), but as a viewer I would, of course, like this story to go on for ever.

1️⃣ The women, just as I thought, have taken matters into their own hands. And, surprisingly, it’s the mothers who turned out to do the most harm — Natasha’s and Roma’s. The first brought home a killer worse than our main characters: the prison governor. As a result, he overheard Natasha’s confidences about the money, which, for some reason (good grief, after all her mother’s abuse and the business with the abortion?!), she had actually brought along as well. And the second was so impressed by Volkov’s warnings about “the old prostitute” that she squirrelled her son’s money away for good, who knows where (my guess is that, in the simplicity of her heart, she may well have handed it to that same Kostya Volkov “for safekeeping”).

2️⃣ Kostya Volkov’s daughter does seem to have a soul after all. If Ivan Yankovsky’s character gets wind that his daughter is now lying to him too (and he has already worked it all out from the suitcase and the dirty boots), I wonder what fate awaits her.

3️⃣ Speaking of Volkov. There was so little of him in this episode that I’m more and more convinced he was never there at all. Still, one thing in favour of his existence is the prison governor’s order to deal with Volkov. I think that will be the main attraction of the final episode — who ends up dealing with whom.

4️⃣ The prosecutor’s wife delivered a very interesting character transformation in this episode. I always had a particular soft spot for her, and now, for the brilliant self-organisation of the prison staff’s wives, I respect her even more. It’s just a pity that this looks set to lead to roughly nothing.

27 March 2025

Outsource is over. I haven’t enjoyed a series this much in a long time (not since Give a Show!); to put it briefly, it’s really something (I’m as pleased as Mila Ershova in this still). And to put it at greater length, here are a few thoughts:

  • I kept hoping that Volkov didn’t exist, but the show’s makers went one better and made him immortal! I can’t remember when I was last as happy for any screen character as I was when Volkov turned out to be alive. (All right, it’s all simpler than that: either the convict doctor gave the wrong recipe for the cocktail, which is why he was laughing so hard at the end of the episode, or the prison doctor got it wrong, or the potion simply worked on the gravely ill, and Yankovsky’s healthy character survived it).

  • By the way, I’m also glad that Volkov didn’t carry out the final shooting with his own hands — that would have gone against his character. And now it’s clear, too, what happened at Volkov’s previous job and why he was transferred from it — evidently his former colleagues also failed his simple little test of what they were made of.

  • Hooray, they showed us, if only for a few seconds, our friends’ business collapsing because the death penalty was abolished. On the one hand, as the memes say, it’s a joy — the death penalty is gone, along with the people who turned it into a way of making money. On the other — what else, if we hypothetically continued the series, could prison warders sell to the powers that be once the death penalty has been abolished? Demand creates supply, as we know, so inventors like Volkov will probably never die out either.

  • The women, as usual, are on fire. In this episode, specifically the characters of Mila Ershova (her character’s strut with the Raffaellos has already become a meme) and Darya Kotreleva (a spectacular escape from everyone gave her character perhaps the happiest ending of all). It’s very good that all the female characters, supporting ones though they were, got some clear full stop in the finale or a trajectory for the future — after all, supporting heroines don’t always get that kind of attention.

  • It’s good that the story has partly come full circle — now Kostya Volkov will head off to a new job at another penal colony and stir things up there. That’s even better than a standard ending — now a second season of this story is definitely not needed (unless it’s as an anthology).

28 March 2025

To close the subject of Outsource, I took part in a big Kinopoisk discussion (Kinopoisk is Russia’s largest film database, media and streaming service) of the series finale.

My colleagues’ opposing opinions show just how topical a screen event Outsource really turned out to be. I hope the truth is hiding somewhere in our argument.

1 April 2025

All right, I hadn’t quite finished talking about Outsource after all.

There is an interesting detail in the scene where Mila Ershova saunters grandly, Raffaellos in hand, all the way to the Dolphin bar. When her character walks into the bar, the usual baroque music breaks off and Mumiy Troll (the cult Russian rock band) take over with the song “Dolphins”, specifically its lines about breaking one day and about poison.

What we hear is, essentially, an outright spoiler: very soon Natasha Rybkina will break; to get her husband back, she will poison Volkov (unsuccessfully), and she will be poisoned herself by that same poison of seeming impunity.

The best moment in the show’s entire soundtrack, if you ask me.
