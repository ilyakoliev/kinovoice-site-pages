Why Tantsuy, Seledka! (“Dance, Seledka!”) is our Aftersun (released in Russia as Solntse moyo)

(no, not only because the heroine rummages through her father’s video cassettes for the whole film))

here is a checklist of similarities:

  • a daughter’s last journey with her father: it does not matter that in the case of Seledka this journey is literally a last journey to the morgue, while in Aftersun it is a trip to a resort. What matters is that these are the last hours in her father’s company

  • the soundtrack: really, never mind that “Under Pressure” has turned into Malikov’s “Do zavtra” (“Till Tomorrow”) in our version; the feelings of irreplaceable loss (but with hope for the future) are the same

  • the carpet: in Aftersun that sad carpet, bought in Turkey on holiday, passes to the heroine as an inheritance, while in Seledka a traditional Soviet carpet, practically taken down off the wall, is used to wrap the corpse of Pavel Maykov’s character

  • the cassettes, and my traditional passion for finding references to Assa in everything: we all remember that Aftersun is built on rewatching tapes, while in Tantsuy, Seledka! the heroine receives a cassette her father recorded especially for her, and he even says an introduction at the start of the tape. If you have had a really lousy day, he says, you have to dance. We do not hear what is recorded on the cassette after that (that is how the plot goes), but for some reason I am sure the tape would have had the song “Chudesnaya strana” (“Wonderful Country”), to which Drubich’s heroine sat sadly in someone else’s coat at the end of Assa. Otherwise, what else would you listen to when things get really bad and you have to dance
